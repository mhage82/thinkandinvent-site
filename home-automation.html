Smart Branch-Duct Zoning

Room-by-room heating and cooling for an existing forced-air home, without replacing the furnace, the air conditioner, the thermostat, or the ductwork.

Concept and prototype design

Most homes with central air have one thermostat in one room. Upstairs bedrooms run hot in summer, basements run cold in winter, and the home office is never right.

This project adds a temperature sensor to each room and a motorized, modulating damper to each branch duct where it leaves the main supply trunk in the basement. A controller then shifts air toward the rooms that need it, while watching duct pressure and air temperatures so the furnace and AC are never pushed outside safe limits.

Why the dampers are in the basement

Smart vents replace the register in every room: visible, battery-powered, and placed at the very end of the duct where they have the least control. In many older homes, each room is fed by its own round duct leaving a basement trunk. Putting the damper right at that take-off means:

  • Nothing changes in the living space. The original registers stay.
  • Dampers can be wired, so there are no batteries inside ducts.
  • Fail-safe behavior is predictable. Every damper can be commanded open instantly, which newer A2L-refrigerant equipment requires on a detected leak.

The hard part: air has to go somewhere

A furnace or air conditioner is designed to move a fixed amount of air through the whole duct system. Close too many dampers and:

  • duct pressure rises: the blower works harder, gets louder, and ducts leak more;
  • airflow across the equipment drops: a furnace can overheat and trip its high-limit switch, and an AC coil can freeze.

Lab research by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory found that simply closing registers raised duct pressure and leakage, and in simulation actually increased energy use. That is why this system redistributes air rather than shutting rooms off, and why every damper decision passes through a safety layer first.

Control strategy

Rather than giving each room its own on/off thermostat, the controller treats the house as one system, in four layers:

  1. Hard safety limits: pressure, temperature rise, supply-air low limit, refrigerant leak.
  2. Airflow allocator: every few minutes, choose damper positions that best meet room demands while staying inside those limits.
  3. Room demand: simple per-room models of how fast each room warms or cools.
  4. Damper position: each damper's learned curve converts "this much air" into "this blade angle."

Where machine learning helps, and where it doesn't

  • Useful: fitting each damper's airflow curve and each room's thermal response from normal operation; spotting abnormal HVAC behavior such as a clogging filter, failing blower, or leaking duct.
  • Not needed: the safety logic and the energy objective are known and explicit. A learned model estimates parameters; it never overrides a safety limit.

Register airflow check

Without it, the controller works out which damper feeds which room by closing a damper and waiting for room temperatures to drift. That takes tens of minutes per damper, and an open door or afternoon sun can fool it. A small airflow sensor clipped under each register grille sees the answer in seconds.

What it adds

  • Fast, reliable mapping. Close one damper; the register whose airflow drops belongs to it. Shared branches and rooms with two registers show up automatically, and it works with the fan alone, without heating or cooling.
  • A check on the airflow model. The controller predicts how much air each register should get; the sensor confirms it. That catches a stuck damper, a crushed or disconnected duct, a register someone closed, or furniture blocking a vent.
  • Better safety evidence. If a branch the controller believes is open shows little or no air, it opens other dampers instead of trusting the model.

Reservations

Relative airflow is easy; exact airflow is hard. Air speed across a grille is uneven, and every register style behaves differently. HVAC professionals use a flow hood for exact CFM. Register sensors are good for "which damper feeds this register" and "this branch dropped 40% from its baseline," not for reporting precise room airflow.

  • It brings hardware back into the rooms. Keeping everything in the basement was a main goal. The sensor must stay small and invisible: a clip-on puck under the existing grille, with no motor and nothing changed on the surface.
  • It adds a battery device per register. A home with 15 registers means 15 more batteries to replace and 15 more radios. Measuring only on request, a few times a day, keeps battery life in years.
  • It adds cost per room. Roughly $15–30 of sensor per register in prototype quantities, before the radio, battery and housing.
  • Sensors get dirty or knocked loose. Dust, vacuum cleaners and pets will disturb them. A reading that disagrees with everything else should mark the sensor as suspect, not the duct.
  • A sensor in the register can't measure the room. It sits in supply air. If it's combined with the room sensor, the room temperature has to come from a separate point on the wall, with only a short probe reaching the register.
  • Prior art exists. Older patents cover registers with airflow sensors, including the Kates/Google family (mostly expired) and EcoVent's register-sensor patent (US 9,854,335). Using register sensors to map and verify dampers placed at the basement take-offs looks less covered, but needs a patent attorney's review.

The prototype uses permanent pressure-difference sensors, so real data can show whether the ongoing check catches enough problems to justify its cost, or whether the installer-tool version is enough.

Setup: the system learns the house

The biggest barrier to zoning an existing home is the manual design and balancing work. The goal is for the system to commission itself. With register airflow sensors, the mapping step takes seconds per damper; without them, it falls back on slower room temperature response.

Fail-safe behavior

Every failure ends in the same place: all dampers open, and the original thermostat back in control. The house then behaves exactly as it did before the system was installed.

  • Dampers open on their own if they hear nothing from the controller for about a minute.
  • The controller's relays fail back to direct thermostat pass-through if it loses power.
  • A physical bypass switch disables zoning instantly for a homeowner or HVAC technician.
  • Each damper has a manual lever.
  • Internet loss changes nothing except remote access.

Prototype plan

The first prototype favors speed over polish:

  • Controller: ESP32 board with a relay/opto interface to the 24 V thermostat wiring, a 0–500 Pa differential pressure sensor, and two temperature probes.
  • Dampers: off-the-shelf round balancing dampers fitted with geared motors and magnetic angle sensors.
  • Room sensors: Thread or Zigbee temperature/humidity sensors.
  • Register sensors: small pressure-difference sensors clipped under each grille, with a thermistor for supply-air detection, measuring only when the controller asks.
  • Software: ESPHome and Home Assistant for the first version, moving the control loop onto the controller once the logic is proven.

Design principles

  1. Do no harm to the HVAC equipment. Pressure and temperature limits come before comfort.
  2. Redistribute, don't shut off. Air is steered, never trapped.
  3. Fail open. Any fault returns the house to its original behavior.
  4. Local first. The house keeps working without the internet.
  5. No lock-in. Any 24 V thermostat and equipment; open integrations.

Background research for this project included manufacturer documentation for existing zoning products and smart vents, ACCA Manual Zr (the residential zoning design standard), Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's register-closing study, a 2022 Minnesota Department of Commerce study on residential zoning energy savings, and UL 60335-2-40 requirements for A2L refrigerants.
